Change pulls against continuity

Societies are always pulled two ways.

Change

An alteration in society's ideas, behaviours, institutions or structures over time.

Continuity

The persistence of traditions, values and institutions: the stability that holds society together.

When people are ready

Change takes hold when people are dissatisfied with the present, aware of alternatives and willing to act.

Three factors that drive change

Individuals

Inventors, leaders and thinkers whose ideas and actions spark change.

Physical environment

Climate, disasters, resources and geography force adaptation, e.g., drought causing migration.

Technology

New tools reshape work, communication and daily life: printing press, internet, smartphones.

How fast, how, and how big change is

Evolutionary change is slow and gradual, building up over a long time. Revolutionary change is rapid and dramatic.

What a social movement needs

A movement is a sustained, organized effort for social change. Three ingredients usually line up.

Political opportunity

The system is open to change: allies in power, weak opposition, or a crisis that opens a window.

Engaged individuals

Committed people willing to join, donate, protest and spread the message.

Organization and infrastructure

Leaders, funding and communication networks that keep it going.

Reinforcement theory (Skinner)

Positive reinforcement

Add a reward → behaviour increases.

Negative reinforcement

Remove something unpleasant → behaviour increases.

Punishment

Behaviour decreases.

Extinction

Reinforcement stops → behaviour fades.

Collective behaviour and social movements

Two forces drive social change: collective behaviour and social movements.

Collective behaviour

Voluntary, often spontaneous activity by large numbers of people that typically violates dominant norms and values.

Institutionalized behaviour

Guided by firmly established norms: predictable, like going to school or work.

Social movement

A sustained, organized collective effort for social change. Slide example: Black Lives Matter.

Ethnocentrism, cultural lag and inequality

Ethnocentrism

Using one's own culture, ethnicity or group as the standard to judge others. The opposite, cultural relativism, judges a culture by its own standards †.

Cultural lag

The gap when material culture (technology, tools) changes faster than non-material culture (laws, norms, values). Example †: AI advancing faster than rules for using it.

Social movements, deviance and collective behaviour are born out of unfairness or inequality in income, education and health care.
Inequality→Strain and frustration→Deviance, collective behaviour, movements
If no one addresses these issues: movements could become obsolete and powerless as the rich keep the poor down. Support this thesis with conflict theory and Smelser's social control.

Deviance: Durkheim, Marx and labelling

Deviance

Behaviour that violates social norms, values or expectations. It varies between societies because norms differ from culture to culture.

Émile Durkheim

Anomie: a breakdown of social norms and values. An act is criminal because it shocks the collective conscience.

Karl Marx

Alienation: disconnection from work, society, others and oneself.

Labelling theory

People become deviant because of the labels placed on them. Stigma is a powerful negative status that affects self-concept, identity and interactions.

Labelled deviant→Stigma→Self-concept changes→Treated differently

Merton's strain theory: five adaptations

Society sets goals (success, wealth) without always giving the means (education, a job). The gap creates strain, which can lead to deviance.

AdaptationGoalsMeansExample †
ConformityAcceptAcceptStudy hard, get a good job
